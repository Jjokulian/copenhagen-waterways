# Where the water goes

Generated by `scripts/currents.py`. Currents from the Open-Meteo Marine API (CMEMS global physics, hourly, 2022-2026); wind and rain from the ERA5 archive (hourly, 1995-2025). Both free and keyless. Companion to [SEABED.md](#SEABED.md), which computes what the wind lifts off the bed; this asks where it then goes.

## First, what this data cannot do

The only free current field covering Danish waters is a global model on a roughly 9 km grid. The Danish straits are narrower than that. Before using it for anything, here is the peak speed it produces against published values:

| Point | Model peak | Published peak | Ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| koege_bugt | 0.50 m/s | 0.4 m/s | 0.8× |
| oresund_s | 0.89 m/s | 1.5 m/s | 1.7× |
| drogden | 0.89 m/s | 1.7 m/s | 1.9× |
| oresund_n | 0.81 m/s | 1.5 m/s | 1.9× |
| fehmarn | 1.08 m/s | 1.2 m/s | 1.1× |
| storebaelt | 0.86 m/s | 1.8 m/s | 2.1× |

The open-water points are about right; the narrow straits come out roughly half the real speed, which is what a 9 km grid does to a 4 km channel. **So nothing below uses an absolute speed.** Everything is a ratio, a sign, or a timing, and each is stated with the reason it survives the calibration error.

## The result: Køge Bugt does not flush

The metric is the **residual current divided by the mean speed** — how much of the water's motion actually goes somewhere. A factor-of-two error in speed cancels out of a ratio of two speeds, so this number survives the problem above.

| Point | Mean speed | Residual | Residual heading | Flushing time over 20 km |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| koege_bugt | 0.067 m/s | 0.0032 m/s | 142° | **73 days** |
| storebaelt | 0.180 m/s | 0.0101 m/s | 296° | **23 days** |
| sydfynske | 0.096 m/s | 0.0265 m/s | 63° | **9 days** |
| arkona | 0.138 m/s | 0.0351 m/s | 74° | **7 days** |
| fehmarn | 0.273 m/s | 0.0527 m/s | 342° | **4 days** |
| oresund_n | 0.220 m/s | 0.0565 m/s | 22° | **4 days** |
| oresund_s | 0.180 m/s | 0.0638 m/s | 43° | **4 days** |
| drogden | 0.180 m/s | 0.0638 m/s | 43° | **4 days** |
| aarhus_bugt | 0.125 m/s | 0.0676 m/s | 36° | **3 days** |

**Køge Bugt takes 73 days to move water 20 km. Aarhus Bugt takes 3.4.** A factor of 22. Køge Bugt has both the lowest mean speed in the set and the smallest residual: the water moves constantly and ends up where it started.

*One caveat, applied honestly.* A low residual can also mean a strong reversing flow with little net — which is what the Great Belt's 23 days is. The Belt has 2.7× Køge Bugt's mean speed; it exchanges water vigorously and just happens to average out. Køge Bugt is slow *and* net-zero. Those are different states and only the second is retention.

### Which inverts the monitoring

Aarhus Bugt registers oxygen depletion every year. Køge Bugt is recorded as having none, including in 2023 and 2025. And Aarhus Bugt flushes twenty times faster.

That is not a contradiction, because the two things are not the same phenomenon. DCE's iltsvind criterion is dissolved oxygen below 4 mg/l in **stratified bottom water** — which requires depth and a sealed layer, and Aarhus Bugt has both. Køge Bugt is shallow and mixes, so it cannot qualify however bad it gets. But retention of surface material, floating mats and fine sediment is a *different* property, and on that one Køge Bugt is the extreme case in this set.

The national instrument measures the property Køge Bugt does not have, and does not measure the property it has more of than anywhere else here.

**Det Sydfynske Øhav** comes out mid-range at 8.7 days — retentive, but nothing like Køge Bugt. It does register iltsvind. On this evidence the two bays are not the same case, and the shared vortex description is not supported by this model. A 9 km grid cannot resolve an archipelago, so this is weak evidence either way.

## A negative result: the wind index does not work

The plan was to regress the strait flow on a wind-driven Baltic filling index, and if it held, use the 31-year wind record to extend the 4-year current record. It does not hold.

Best correlation is **r = 0.44 at a 24 hour window**, with sign agreement of only **61%** over 34,968 hours. Correlation decays monotonically at every longer window — 168 h gives 0.17, 336 h gives 0.01.

A 24-hour optimum is a local synoptic wind response, not a basin filling and draining over weeks. Either the multi-week Baltic memory is not the dominant control on the Sound, or this coarse product cannot see it. Reported as a negative result: **the 31-year extension is not available on this data.** Everything about currents here is four years long.

## Does Copenhagen's overflow water go toward Køge Bugt?

Copenhagen discharges into the harbour and the southern Sound. Køge Bugt is south of that, and the mean flow runs north. So the claim is not about averages: it requires that the **specific hours in which the city overflows** are hours in which the water runs south. The rain record and the current record overlap for 35,064 hours, so this is directly testable.

Baseline: the southern Sound runs south **42%** of all hours.

| 6-hour rainfall | Hours | Runs south | vs baseline |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| dry | 23,425 | 42.7% | +0.7 pp |
| light | 9,099 | 43.0% | +1.0 pp |
| moderate | 1,695 | 35.0% | -7.0 pp |
| heavy | 604 | 30.1% | -11.8 pp |
| overflow-scale | 134 | 23.9% | -18.1 pp |

**During the rain, the water runs north.** The harder it rains, the more strongly — at overflow-scale rainfall, southward transport is 18 points *below* baseline. Which is physically obvious once seen: heavy rain in Copenhagen arrives with cyclonic southwesterlies, and those drive the Sound north.

Taken alone that refutes the transport claim. But an overflow plume does not stop moving when the rain stops:

| Hours after the event | Runs south | vs baseline |
|---|---:|---:|
| +0 h | 22.1% | **-19.9 pp** |
| +6 h | 44.1% | **+2.2 pp** |
| +12 h | 59.3% | **+17.3 pp** ← |
| +24 h | 47.6% | **+5.6 pp** |
| +48 h | 51.0% | **+9.1 pp** |
| +72 h | 37.2% | **-4.7 pp** |

**Twelve hours after an overflow-scale event, southward transport runs 59% — +17 points above baseline.** That is the post-frontal wind veer: the front passes, the wind swings to the northwest, and the water reverses while the plume is still in it.

So the mechanism is neither the simple claim nor its refutation. It is a **lag**. The discharge happens under northward flow and the transport reverses roughly half a day later, on a timescale set by the same weather system that caused the discharge.

*Statistical honesty:* this rests on 145 event-hours, and six lags were tested. The 0-hour and 12-hour results are both large and both have the same simple mechanism behind them, which is why they are reported. A longer current record would settle it; four years is what exists.

## A correction to SEABED.md

That document said the resuspension season coincides with the overflow season. Over 31 years of rainfall that is wrong, and the error is worth keeping visible.

| | Peak months |
|---|---|
| Rain intense enough to overflow a combined sewer (≥10 mm in 6 h) | **Jun–Aug**, 4.2–5.1 h/yr; near zero Jan–Apr |
| Total rainfall | flat, 38–72 mm/month, slight Jun–Aug and Oct maxima |
| Wave-driven bed resuspension | **Oct–Jan**, roughly double July |
| Strong onshore wind (stranding) | **Oct–Jan** |

Overflow *events* are driven by intensity, and intensity in Denmark is convective, and convection is summer. The autumn is wetter in total but gentler.

This does not weaken the case — it sharpens it into a **deposit-then-mobilise sequence**:

1. **June–August:** cloudbursts overflow the combined system. Sewage solids, fat and basin sludge are discharged into a warm, weakly-mixed, retentive bay.
2. **The bay holds it.** Flushing time is on the order of months, so the material settles locally rather than being exported.
3. **Through late summer:** it decays in place, in the warmest water of the year, with the oxygen demand and the smell that implies.
4. **October–January:** the gales arrive. The bed — now looser for having been anoxic — resuspends twice as often as in July, and the same wind that lifts it drives it onto the western shore.

Delivery in summer and arrival on the shore in autumn are not in conflict. They are the two ends of a months-long residence time, and the residence time is the measured part.

Small numbers, and they should be read as small. The joint condition is rare — which is the point. If the transport to Køge Bugt happens in a handful of hours a year, then an annual-average accounting cannot represent it at all, and neither can a monitoring programme that samples on a calendar.

## What is still missing

- **A current field that resolves the straits.** CMEMS publishes a Baltic regional reanalysis at roughly 1 km behind free registration. Everything here would be worth redoing on it, and the retention result is the one to re-test first.
- **Particle tracking.** Retention time is a summary statistic. Where material released off Amager actually ends up needs trajectories, which needs the field above.
- **More than four years.** The wind index failed, so there is no way to extend the current record backwards, and the event counts stay small.
- **Density.** The Sound is strongly stratified — Baltic water out on top, Kattegat water in underneath. A single depth-averaged current hides that a plume and the bottom water can be going opposite ways at the same hour.
