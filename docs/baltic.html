The Baltic below the halocline

Oxygen at the Gotland Deep, central Baltic — roughly 500 km east of Denmark, in Swedish waters. Depth is gridded to one metre here. The Danish station archive collapses the same axis to two bins, so this is the dimension that view cannot show — at the cost, stated below, that the fine vertical structure is interpolated rather than measured.

It is here because of a question with a clean logical shape: if dead water were a Danish phenomenon, the cause would be contained in Denmark. The Danish station view is here.

Key — what every mark means

One cell — one month, one metre of depth. 226 profiles × 240 levels. Time runs left to right, depth downward.
Near-black → brown → orange → tan → blue — low to high. For oxygen the dark end is anoxic and the blue end is well-oxygenated, so the dark band is the dead layer and its upper edge is the oxycline.
Flat background colour — no profile that month. Not zero, and not interpolated across: 159 of 385 months are blank, nearly all of them before 2006.
Horizontal rules — depth at 0, 50, 100, 150, 200 and 240 m, labelled at the left.
Numbers along the bottom — years, every fifth. Spacing is per profile, not per calendar month, so thin years occupy the same width as dense ones.

Switch the selector to salinity and the same plot shows the halocline: a sharp horizontal boundary at about the same depth as the top of the dark oxygen band. That coincidence is the whole finding.

What the record says

Every month of the record, at every depth below 100 m, is under 2 mg/l. Not seasonal, not episodic.

Four things to read off it

1. The oxygen boundary is the salinity boundary. The top of the <2 mg/l layer sits at a median of −73 m; the halocline, taken as the steepest salinity gradient, sits at −69 m. They are the same surface. Switch the selector to salinity and you are looking at the cause. A density gradient, not a nutrient gradient.

2. It does not move. Across five-year blocks the boundary sits at −75, −75, −73, −72, −71 m and the halocline at −65 to −70 m. No expansion, no contraction, over the whole usable record.

3. One event interrupts it, and it decays. Oxygen at −200 m: 0.05 mg/l in 2014, 1.38 in 2015, 1.11 in 2016, 0.36 in 2017, back to 0.05 in 2018. That is the 2014–16 Major Baltic Inflow — dense North Sea water pushed through the Danish straits by sustained westerly wind. It is the only thing in 30 years that reoxygenated the deep basin, it is weather, and it was gone in three years.

4. The record is not as long as it looks. 226 of 385 months carry a profile, and every year from 1993 to 2005 has three or fewer. The dense record is 2006–2024. The left third of this plot is thin data drawn wide.

The severity runs opposite to the attention. Two things are being called by one word: permanent basin anoxia beneath a fixed halocline, and seasonal shallow coastal dead water. They are not the same mechanism, and a policy calibrated on one is measured in the other.

Provenance

Copernicus Marine OMI, in-situ observations reprocessed — not a reanalysis. An earlier version of this page said reanalysis and was wrong; the file's own attributes read "from Observations Reprocessing", source INSITU_BAL_...DISCRETE.... So these are real profiles, taken by Swedish, Estonian and Latvian programmes rather than Danish ones — a genuinely different set of ships, operators and laboratories, though the same class of instrument.

But: "measurements have been regridded on a regular grid with monthly timestep and 1 m vertical step". The vertical smoothness is interpolation between discrete samples, not 240 independent measurements per profile. Class 3, theirs, and stated in their own metadata.
